Field Manual

The complete reference for RUMPUS ENGINE — every tab, section, fold and slider in the level editor, plus the systems behind racing, vehicles, signals, multiplayer and sharing. For guided walkthroughs with one-click example projects, open Help & tutorials on the game's home menu instead.

BasicsGetting started

RUMPUS ENGINE is a single-file browser game studio — build worlds, play them (FPS, racing, top-down, side-scroll), and share them. Nothing installs; the whole game is one page.

The home menu

  • Deploy — play the current level (your autosaved work, a loaded file, or the default arena). Its equal twin Build / edit level opens the editor on the same level.
  • Multiplayer — host or join a room (see Multiplayer). Community levels — browse and play what other creators have published.
  • Play campaign / Load campaign — play a sequence of chained levels with carry-over loadout (in the footer strip).
  • Instructions — a quick controls card. Help & tutorials — guided tutorials with loadable example projects. Field manual — this page. Credits — engine libraries plus the asset attributions collected for the current level.
The build number is shown at the bottom-left of the home menu. If a fix doesn't seem to be live, hard-refresh (Ctrl+Shift+R) and check that number first.

Gamepads and touch are supported: plug in a controller (press ] to switch between detected pads), or on a phone use the on-screen joysticks and drive cluster — pause → ✿ Customize controls to move or resize any on-screen button.

Rebinding: pause → ⌨ Keyboard bindings to remap movement and every action (click an action, press the new key; picking a key already in use swaps the two; Reset restores defaults). Weapon slots 1–5, Esc and the editor's own keys stay fixed, and on-screen prompts keep showing the default keys.

Controller menus: every menu is playable from the pad — D-pad or left stick moves the focus ring, A selects, B backs out, and left/right tunes sliders and dropdowns. The first press lands on Deploy.

Controller: Start pauses (solo) or opens the match menu (multiplayer). D-pad: up = inventory, down = map, left = melee, right = flashlight. In the supply cache, D-pad picks, A buys, B/Y closes; on the upgrade screen, bumpers or D-pad focus a card and A takes it (keyboard: press 1–3). Look sensitivity, aim sensitivity, stick deadzone and invert-Y live in the pause menu once a pad is connected.

Touch: besides the joysticks — CROUCH toggles, MELEE swings, BUILD opens the deploy radial (tap a slice to place), and hold USE to grab or drop a physics prop. LIGHT, BAG and CHAT appear automatically when a level has a flashlight, an inventory, or you're in a multiplayer room.

Motion, comfort & accessibility

Pause → Comfort. These belong to you, not to the level — they follow you into every level including ones other people made, which is the point: a creator cannot be relied upon to have thought about it. Every slider is a percentage of what the level authored, so 100% is exactly as shipped and nothing moves unless you move it.

SettingWhat it turns down
Camera shakeEvery kick — gunfire, explosions, impacts, landings.
Camera swayThe lean and dip the body gives the camera as you move.
Motion blurScales whatever the level authored, without editing the level.
Damage flashThe red pulse when you are hit. At 0 it still shows a faint tint — being hit always stays visible, because that is information, not decoration.
Slow-mo on killsThe brief time-stop on a kill or a multi-kill.

Interface size75–175%. Scales the whole interface and its click targets, so a bigger HUD is not a HUD you cannot press. On-screen touch controls are deliberately exempt — they have their own layout editor.
Colour visionOff / Protanopia (red) / Deuteranopia (green) / Tritanopia (blue), with a Correction strength dial. This is a correction, not a simulation: it takes the colour difference your eye cannot carry and redistributes it onto the channels that can. Neutral greys never move.

Reduce all motion turns the lot down in one press; Restore defaults puts them back. If your operating system already asks for reduced motion, RUMPUS ENGINE starts calm without you having to say so again — and an explicit choice always wins after that, including the choice to turn it all back up.

A photosensitivity notice appears once per browser at first launch, with a Reduce flashing button that drives these same sliders. The Photosensitivity warning button in the panel brings it back on demand.

Editor referenceBuild tab

Add shape

+ Box, + Sphere, + Cylinder, + Cone, + Ramp, + Stairs, + Dome, + Tube, + Ring drop a primitive in front of the camera. Every shape gets the full Material editor. The first four use exact box/sphere colliders; the construction shapes (Ramp/Stairs/Dome/Tube/Ring) collide with their real triangles, so you can walk and drive up a ramp or staircase, shelter under a dome (it's hollow — visible from inside), run through a tube, or hop through a ring. Like every primitive they're authored to a 1×1×1 footprint with the base at floor level, so scaling and floor-snapping behave exactly like the box: stretch a Ramp long and low for a drivable slope, or scale Stairs up for building-sized steps. A Pillar and the track pieces are also primitives under the hood.

Room

Object & selection → Room. A doorway used to be four boxes you placed and measured by hand. This builds the whole shell — floor, four walls, optional ceiling, and every opening cut through them — as ordinary props you can then edit, move, texture and delete one at a time.

FieldWhat it means
Width (X) / Depth (Z)The inside measurement. Type 8 × 6 and you get 8 × 6 of floor — not 8 minus the walls. Interior-first is the only measurement that means anything when you are placing furniture in it.
HeightFloor to ceiling, inside.
Wall thicknessHow thick the walls are, built outward from the interior.
Floor slab / CeilingTick either off for an open-top room or a shell sitting on terrain you already have.
The openings listEach row is one hole: which wall, how far along (at), its width, its sill height off the floor, and its height. Sill 0 is a doorway; sill 1 is a window. ✕ removes one.

Build room drops it where you are looking and tells you how many props it made. Undo removes the whole room in one press.

Make doorways at least 1.8 m wide. The player is 1.6 m across, so a 1.6 m door is a coin flip on whether they fit — and enemies pathing through need the room too. The default is 2.0 m and the panel warns you below 1.8.
A room is props, not a special object. That is the whole point: the gizmo, snapping, materials, duplication, prefabs, tags and signals all work on every piece afterwards. Build a corridor by duplicating a room and snapping it against the first.

Path — wires & rails

Object & selection → Path (wires & rails). Select two or more props in the order you want them strung, pick a mode, and press the button. Power cables between poles, telephone wire across a street, a fence, a kerb, a catwalk handrail.

ModeFieldsWhat you get
⚡ WireThickness, Sag, Above the pole, Segments per spanA drooping cable. Sag is the droop at the middle of each span, in metres — a number you can see rather than a tension coefficient. It is decoration only, so nothing walks into it.

The path is your selection order, so every selection feature already works here: shift-click the poles one at a time, or marquee them and reorder by re-clicking. Undo removes the whole run.

Track builder

Snap-together race track pieces — covered in depth in Track builder & racing. With pieces placed, the Track appearance panel restyles the whole course at once: road colour, roughness and an optional road texture (with a tiles dial), painted-line colour, the kerb stripe pair, and the barrier colour. Saved with the level; Reset to stock returns the classic asphalt look.

Search free models

One search box finds free 3D models on Poly Pizza (no key needed) and Sketchfab (needs a free API token — enter it once, everywhere else shows “Token saved · change”). Click a result to place it. Every searched asset is recorded for the level's Credits screen automatically.

Object & selection

With something selected, this section shows: swap/replace model, and the eight folds that give a prop behaviour — animation, locks, signals, dialogue, vehicle, joints, mechanisms, physics & destruction.

Material

For shape primitives: colour, texture map with U/V tiling and rotation, shininess, metalness, emissive glow — and Opacity. Opacity below 1 makes the shape see-through: glass = low opacity + high shininess, frosted plastic = mid opacity + low shininess, and a faint pane at the minimum (0.15) reads as a force field. A transparent prop stays completely solid — you can stand on a glass floor and bullets still hit it. Opacity saves with the level and carries through share links and multiplayer like every other material setting. Imported models keep their own materials — the section says so instead of hiding.

Tiling on a stretched shape. A texture on a primitive is measured in metres, not per face — so stretching a box into a long thin wall keeps the grain the same size everywhere instead of squashing it on some faces and repeating it hundreds of times on others. The Tile number is tiles across the shape's longest side, which is what it already meant for the biggest face, so that face looks exactly as it always did. There is one density in both directions now, so the second field is off.

Stretch to fit puts the old behaviour back for that prop: one tile per face whatever its real size. Useful when you want a texture to follow a shape — a label wrapped on a sign, a decal sized to a panel.

Transform & Handles

Transform is the numeric version of the gizmo: exact position, rotation and scale fields. Handles pick the gizmo mode (move / rotate / scale — also 1/2/3) and proportional scaling (4).

Taper — pinching one end of a box

Scale stretches a shape evenly. Taper pinches one end of a box, which is the thing three scale numbers can never do. Select a box and the Taper rows appear at the bottom of the Transform fold.

Pinch the picks which end: the top (+Y), the right end (+X) or the far end (+Z). The two sliders under it scale that end on the two axes across it — 1 leaves it alone, 0 pinches it to nothing, and anything above 1 flares it outward. The end you did not pick is never touched, and the shape keeps its base on the floor, so nothing you have already placed moves.

Step by step, for the dart shape:

  1. Add a box and scale it into a long thin slab — say 1 × 1 × 20.
  2. Open Transform and set Pinch the to far end (+Z).
  3. Pull the first slider (X at that end) down to 0. Both corners of that end come together and you have a point.

The same two numbers give you a lot of shapes:

ShapePinch theSliders
Pyramid / spiketop (+Y)0 and 0
Roof, or a wedge you can drive uptop (+Y)1 and 0
Obelisk, buttress, tapered columntop (+Y)0.4 and 0.4

It is a real solid, not a trick of the light. Physics, bullets, footsteps and shadows all follow the pinched shape — you can stand on the slope of a tapered roof and a shot at the point of a dart passes beside it rather than hitting an invisible box. The one cost: tapered boxes are drawn individually rather than batched with their plain neighbours, so use them for the handful of shapes that need it rather than for a field of five hundred.

Groups

Every prop's Tag now sits at the top of Object & selection (and stays in the Signals fold — they edit the same tag). Ctrl+Shift+C collapses every editor section at once.

Outliner (scene hierarchy)

Press O (or the list button on the editor toolbar) for the Outliner — the whole placed scene as one panel: props, lights, enemy spawns, turrets and pickups, each with its tag. As levels grow this is the fastest way around:

  • Search matches names, tags and folder names; the tag dropdown narrows the list to one tag.
  • Folders: select some props, then use the Move → dropdown in the Props group to file them into a folder (or create a new one) — or just drag a row onto a folder (drop on the Props header to un-file it). Folders save with the level. Double-click a folder to rename it; a folder's eye/padlock hides or locks everything inside at once.
  • Eye hides an object while editing only — deploys and play always show everything. Great for getting a roof out of the way.
  • Padlock locks an object: viewport clicks and marquee boxes pass straight through it, so a big backdrop stops stealing every click. A locked object can't be selected or moved at all until you click its padlock again.
  • Select all of a type: the grid button on a type header (or a folder) selects everything it contains — filter first to select just that slice (e.g. every prop tagged door).
  • Double-click a prop or light to name it; names save with the level and make search actually useful.

Select multiple props and press Ctrl+G to group them — the group moves, rotates, scales and duplicates as one. Ctrl+Shift+G ungroups.

Prefabs

Turn any configured selection into a reusable component — a door with its trigger, an elevator, a trap, a whole furnished room. Select the props, type a name in the Prefabs section and hit Save selection: the capture keeps everything — signals, mechanism animation, physics and joints, vehicle tuning, locks, dialogue, materials. Place drops a copy 8 m ahead of the camera; every copy arrives grouped (moves as one) and heights are terrain-relative, so a room built on flat ground sits correctly on a hillside.

Your prefab library lives in your browser and follows you across levels — and every level embeds the prefabs it uses, so a shared or community level brings its components along (they join the library of whoever opens it, never overwriting same-named prefabs of their own).

Editing a prefab: change any placed copy — move parts, retag, recolor, even add new props to its group — select it and press Update on that prefab's row. The definition rewrites and every other copy in the level follows. Parts you deliberately changed on one specific copy (a raised panel, a renamed tag) are detected as local overrides and left exactly as you authored them — the toast reports how many were kept. Moving a whole copy around the map never counts as an override. Detach disconnects a copy so future updates skip it; × removes a prefab from your library (placed copies stay in their levels).

Editor referenceObject folds — giving props behaviour

Select any prop on the Build tab and open Object & selection. Eight folds, in panel order. (Signals and Mechanism get their full treatment in Signals, locks & mechanisms; Vehicle in Vehicle tuning.)

Animation

Plays a model's built-in clips. Pick the clip (or all clips at once), then when it plays — Auto (from level load), E Activate (player interacts), or Signals only (waits to be targeted) — and how: ⟳ Loop, ⇄ Ping-pong, or → Once. Built-in shapes have no clips (use Mechanism to move those); for models without animations, the Sketchfab search has an "Animated only" filter.

Signs — text in the world

Add a Sign from the shape list (Build tab → Add a shape). It is an ordinary prop — gizmo, snapping, duplication, prefabs, tags all work on it — that draws text on a board. Room labels, booth names, a warning over a door, a scoreboard on a wall.

ControlWhat it does
The text boxWhat the sign says. Multiple lines are fine.
SizeText size on the board (8–200). Long text is shrunk to fit rather than overflowing, so a sign never spills.
TextText colour.
BoardBoard colour, then its opacity — 0 leaves floating text with no board behind it, which is how you get a painted-on-the-wall look.

A sign can show a live number. Curly braces read a Logic variable exactly as a HUD widget does, so Hits: {score} on a plate behind your shooting range is a scoreboard you can see from across the level. It repaints only when the variable changes, so a hundred static labels cost nothing.
A sign is double-sided, so it is never invisible from the wrong side — but the back reads mirrored. Place two back to back if both sides matter. It also defaults to No collision, so it is not an invisible wall in front of a booth; untick that if you want a solid board.

Parenting — props that ride other props

Select the prop that should carry the others last (it becomes the primary), then press Parent to this prop. The children now follow it: a crate rides a moving platform, a lamp travels with the wagon, a whole assembly turns as one when the parent rotates. Their colliders move with them, so the player really does stand on the crate while the lift climbs.

Deleting a parent releases its children where they stand rather than taking them with it. Nothing is lost by parenting something.

Prop values — a counter on the prop itself

Logic tab → Do action → Set prop value. Every prop can hold its own named numbers, so “this plate has been hit 3 times” lives on the plate rather than in a level-wide variable you would need one of per plate. Pick Add or Set, a name, and a value. Leave the tag blank and it writes to the prop whose signal fired the graph — so one node serves every plate in the booth.

Lock

Lock picks the required key (red / blue / gold / green); pressing E on the prop then needs that key in hand — place a matching key pickup pad somewhere findable. Consume key on unlock spends the key (for one-key-one-door puzzles; off = a master key). A prop unlocks once per deploy, for everyone in MP.

Rig an imported model

A model you import is usually just geometry — no skeleton, so the animation editor can only move it as one piece. Select the prop and hit 🦴 Auto-rig model (T-pose) (in the Animation fold, beside the animation editor button): drop a marker on eight joints — chin, groin, both elbows, both wrists, both knees — and RUMPUS ENGINE builds a full humanoid skeleton and skins the mesh to it. After that the animation editor keyframes its arms, legs and spine like any character, and every custom clip in the level retargets onto it. It's the usual way to turn an imported character into an NPC.

The rig belongs to the model, not to one copy. Props share one loaded copy of each model, so the markers are stored against the model's URL — every copy of that shopkeeper stands up the same way, and the level saves one marker set however many you placed. Copies already standing in the scene pick up the new skeleton when the level reloads; a fresh one is rigged the moment it spawns. The same rigger is on the Player tab and the Enemies tab for those models. Works best on a model standing in a T-pose or A-pose.

Dialogue (NPC)

Name (shown over the prompt) + one line of dialogue per row (up to 120 × 200 chars); the player presses E to advance. The first line also fires the prop's On E signals — so a conversation can set the objective, drop a checkpoint, or open a door as it starts.

Replies, conditions & jumps

That's still all a simple NPC needs. When you want the player to answer, six marks turn the same box into a branching conversation — nothing else about it changes, and a script written before this still reads exactly the same way.

MarkWhat it does
> text -> labelA reply button on the row above it. Up to six; the player clicks one, taps it, or presses 1–6. -> end finishes the conversation. Leave the arrow off and it just carries on to the next row. While replies are showing, E does nothing — the player has to answer.
#labelNames the next row so a reply can jump to it. Not a line of dialogue itself.
[if expr]At the start of a row: skip that row unless it's true. At the end of a reply: hide that reply unless it's true — a locked option simply isn't offered.
-> labelJump instead of falling through into the next branch. Write it at the end of a line (I felt sassy once. -> end) or on a row of its own — both work. -> end stops the conversation there. It only counts as a jump when it's a single bare label at the very end of the row, so an arrow used as punctuation mid-sentence is left alone.
{set name = 2}Write a Logic variable when the row is shown or the reply is picked. + and − work too: {set coins + 1}.
{event doorOpen}Fire a Logic event — the same pulse an On event node catches. This and {set} are the bridge: a conversation can drive your whole graph.

A condition compares two things with == != < <= > >=. Either side can be a number, a Logic variable, or item:someId — how many of that inventory item the player is carrying. [if hasKey] with no operator means “it has been set at all”. An unset variable reads as 0, so a condition is never comparing against nothing.

Have you found the brass key?
> Here it is. -> gotIt [if item:brassKey >= 1]
> Still looking. -> notYet
> Leave. -> end
#gotIt
Then the vault is yours. {set questStage = 2} {event vaultOpen} -> end
#notYet
[if questStage >= 1] You said that last time.
Keep looking. It glints in the dark. -> end
Every branch needs an ending. Without one, a branch runs straight on into the next #label below it — so picking the first reply plays every answer in the file. End each one with -> end (or a jump). The editor watches for exactly this and warns you under the box, along with any jump pointing at a #label that doesn't exist.

Rows that start with a reply and have no line above them render as a bare choice prompt — useful for “what do you do?”. A label that doesn't exist ends the conversation rather than looping. Everything the player sees is escaped, so a shared level can't inject markup through a line or a button. Pair this with values that carry over and an NPC can remember you between levels.

Environment

Movement — and the four traversal verbs

Global player physics: Walk speed (4–60) · Run speed (4–100) · Crouch speed (1–40) · Jump (0–30) · Launch power (0.25–4, jump-pad multiplier) · Gravity (5–80) · Eye height (0.4–20).

Four more sliders in the same fold give the player extra ways to move. All four are off at 0, which is exactly how every level built before them plays — turn one on and it is available for the whole level. Each is one number that both enables and tunes the verb, and all four are the jump key except the dash, so there is nothing new to teach a player and rebinding jump moves them together.

SliderRangeWhat it gives the player
Tap hop0.1–1How much of a jump you keep when you release the key on the way up. 1 = off (every jump is full height). At 0.5 a tap is a quarter-height hop and a held key is the full jump — the platformer's short hop. Height goes as the square of speed, so this one number spans the whole tap-to-hold range. A demanding jump is cleared by holding, which is what a player does naturally when reaching for something.
Air jumps0–3How many extra jumps you get in mid-air. Press jump again while airborne. It sets your upward speed rather than adding to it, so a double jump rescues a bad fall instead of merely nudging it. Refunded the moment you touch the ground or grab a ledge, and it inherits the ordinary jump's half-second cooldown, so it cannot be mashed.
Wall jump0, or 3–30Push-off speed when you press jump in mid-air next to a wall. You get the normal jump height plus a shove away from the wall, and your speed along the wall is kept — so running a wall and kicking off it carries your momentum. You cannot take two in a row off the same wall, which is what stops a single wall being an infinite ladder out of a sealed arena; touching the ground, a ledge, or a wall facing a different way arms it again. A shaft with walls on both sides gives no push (there is no direction to pick) — that is deliberate, not a bug.
Air dash0–40A burst of horizontal speed in the direction you are moving, once per airborne trip, on its own key. Refunded on landing.
Building a movement course? Turn on Tap hop (0.5) and Air jumps (1) first — those two alone change how every gap in your level reads, so tune your jump distances after setting them, not before. Add Wall jump when you want vertical routes: two walls facing each other let a player climb, because each kick re-arms the other wall.

Camera & view

Field of view (50–110) · View distance (60–2000 — the hard cutoff where geometry stops drawing; raise it for big racing arenas) · Fog density (lower = see farther into the dark) & colour · ground grid toggle. Depth of field: Focus distance = how far away stays sharp, In-focus range = the depth kept sharp around it, Blur strength = how soft everything else goes. Post-processing: Bloom + Bloom threshold (how bright a surface must be before it glows), Motion blur (trail strength), Vignette, Film grain, Saturation, Contrast — they all compose, DoF included, and they save with the level (the on/off switch is each player's preference).

Cull below (px) stops drawing a prop once it covers fewer than that many pixels on screen — the single biggest performance lever a creator has on a dense level, and it is 0 (off) by default because a setting that removes your content should be your decision, not the engine's. On a scattered 600-prop field, 2 cut draw calls by 57% and cost nothing visible; on a level of 60 big props it does nothing at all, so try it and watch the counters rather than assuming. Nothing within 40 m is ever culled whatever the setting, and a prop carrying a light is never culled — so it can shrink your frame cost but it cannot make a lamp go out or delete the thing you are standing next to. Save ▸ Level check reports what it removed while it is on.

Making a big level run well

Three numbers decide whether a level is playable on ordinary hardware, and Save ▸ Level check reports all three so you do not have to guess:

Floor & terrain

Floor colour / roughness / metalness, then three texture rows — the image itself, an optional normal map and an optional roughness map — with tile W/H and rotation that govern all three, plus a texture search. Terrain: Height (0–18) and Detail (1–8) with Generate / Regenerate / Flatten, plus a terrain brush painted directly onto the ground — Raise / Lower / Smooth sculpt the height, Paint blends materials, and Scatter plants props. A colour-coded cursor ring follows your pointer showing the brush radius; in Paint mode the inner ring is where full strength ends and the soft edge begins, in Scatter mode it shows the planting spacing.

Normal and roughness maps. The texture row gives the floor its colour; a normal map gives it relief — grout lines, cracks, a weave — without any extra geometry, and a roughness map makes parts of it shinier than others (wet patches, worn tracks). Most free texture sites ship all three beside each other; paste each into its own row. The built-in texture search fills all three at once, which is the quickest way to a real surface. Leave them blank and the engine's own fine grain takes over — a blank row is not a missing step. The boundary walls have the same three rows.

Changing the texture in the top row clears the two below it, and says so: relief measured from one material does not describe another. Set the colour first, then the maps.

Material painting (the Paint brush mode)

Make the base floor your ground material (say, tiled grass), then paint up to three more textures straight onto it — a dirt path, a stone plaza, scorched earth. Pick a Layer, give it a texture (URL or the built-in free-texture search) and a Tiles count, then drag on the floor. It works on flat floors and sculpted terrain alike, and the result saves with the level (share links, gallery, multiplayer).

ControlRangeWhat it does / how to use it
Layer 1 / 2 / 3—Which paint layer the brush writes. A ● marks layers that already have a texture. Where layers overlap, the higher-numbered layer paints over the lower.
Tiles2–120How many times this layer's texture repeats across the arena — match it to your base floor tiling so the scale reads consistently.

Landscape scatter (the Scatter brush mode)

Plant randomized props by dragging: trees, bushes, rocks — anything. Fill up to three slots, either from the free-model search (credited automatically) or with Use selected prop, which clones whatever's selected at its exact scale — including built-in shapes. Each slot has its own Size dial: for searched models it's the real-world size in metres (default 7 — tree height; crank it to 25 for giants), for cloned props it's a multiplier on their captured scale. Each pass drops Density (1–6) props at random spots inside the brush circle, each randomly rotated, varied by Size jitter (±0–60%), and sat on the terrain — a slow drag along a hillside plants a treeline.

Scattered props are normal props: marquee-select to move or delete them, undo works, and they save with the level. Budget note: each searched model adds a draw call — a few dozen trees is fine, hundreds will cost frames. Scattered primitives (rocks from spheres, fence posts from cylinders) are basically free thanks to instancing. Forest recipe: slot 1 = a tree, slot 2 = a second tree variety, slot 3 = a bush; Density 3, Size jitter 0.4, Brush size 15 — then wander the map edge in one slow pass.

Triggers — “when something gets here”

Every other zone is a fixed behaviour: a death zone kills, a jump pad launches. A trigger does nothing by itself — it fires a Logic event and hands the moment to your graph, which is where your game actually lives. Before triggers the only things that could start a chain were match start, a timer, an enemy dying, a wave starting, or the player pressing E on a prop. Now you can say “when the player reaches here.”

ControlWhat it does / how to use it
EventThe Logic event name it fires, e.g. reachedVault. The field shares the graph's event dropdown, so pick a name you already use instead of retyping it — and any name you type here shows up in the On event node's list. A trigger with no event name does nothing, and the panel tells you so.
WhenEnters fires on the frame something crosses in (not every frame it stands there). Leaves is the mirror. Stays in pulses on a repeat interval for as long as something is inside — use it for damage-over-time, a capture point ticking up, or an alarm.
WhoPlayer, Enemy, or Anything. In co-op, “Player” means any living teammate — a door opens for whoever reaches it first, and a downed teammate doesn't hold it open.
Radius / Height / Base YA cylinder, exactly like the other zones: it spans Base Y up to Base Y + Height. Height matters — a short trigger can be jumped over, and a trigger on a balcony ignores the floor below it. Drag the blue dot with the move gizmo.
Repeat everyOnly used by Stays in: seconds between pulses (0.1–60).
Only onceFires at most once per match, whichever mode it's in. Restarting the match re-arms it, so a retry plays the same as a first attempt.

The whole loop, end to end: drop a trigger at the vault door, set its event to reachedVault, then in the Logic tab add On event reachedVault → Show message (or Open door, Spawn, Play cutscene, Set variable — anything the graph can do). Triggers are evaluated by the host in multiplayer, and never while you're editing or paused.

Sides — friendlies, allies and third parties

Not everything that moves has to be hostile. Two controls on a spawn marker decide what a creature is.

Friendly

Tick Friendly and you get a wandering NPC: it patrols its area or route, ignores the player and gunfire, does not count as a hostile, and killing it gives you nothing. It is the villager, the shopkeeper's dog, the crowd at the fairground — motion that makes a place feel inhabited without making it a fight.

A friendly does not hold a wave open: a level full of villagers still advances when the last hostile dies. The On enemy killed logic event still fires when one dies, so you can punish shooting the innocents. Hunt mode behaves as Patrol while a marker is friendly.

Faction

A marker that is not friendly picks a Faction. The rule is simply that different sides fight each other, and the player is Your side:

FactionFightsUse it for
Your sideHostile, Third party, Fourth party. Never you.An escort, a turret crew, a squad that fights alongside you.
Hostile (default)You and the other three.Everything every level built before factions already has — nothing changed.
Third party / Fourth partyEveryone but their own side.Two gangs at war that you can walk into the middle of; wildlife that attacks both.
An ally's kill is not your kill — no score, no lifesteal, no callout — but the loot still drops, so you have to walk over and take it. And an ally with nothing to fight simply patrols; it never falls back to hunting you.
A three-way fight. Place two clusters of markers, one Third party and one Fourth party, on opposite sides of a courtyard, and stand at the edge. They will find each other. Mark the survivors' spawn Hostile and the last wave turns on you.

SystemsVehicle tuning — the deep dive

Any prop can be a car: select it → Object & selection → Vehicle (drivable) → tick Drivable vehicle (this also makes the prop non-dynamic — the car system owns its physics). A units toggle switches every speed field and the in-game speedometer between km/h and mph.

How the handling actually works — three separate systems, not one "handling" number: Braking fires when your throttle opposes your direction of travel: you slow with real stopping distance (an ABS-like ease-off in the last couple of m/s) and only then does reverse engage — you can never snap from forward into reverse. Cornering grip (G) is the lateral force the tyres hold: your achievable turn rate shrinks as speed rises, so corners widen like a real car (understeer) — but steering never drops below 35% of what you ask, so it never "bricks". Flooring the throttle mid-corner spends tyre on acceleration instead of cornering (a traction circle). Grip is how fast your direction of travel chases your heading: low grip = the car slides and drifts; the handbrake cuts it to ~16% for deliberate slides; pushing past ~90% of the cornering limit steps the rear out progressively, and counter-steering recovers grip 1.4× faster.

Hide is invisible AND intangible (an invisible wall is worse than no verb); Move preserves height above the ground, so a crate moved from a ledge to a valley lands on the valley floor; Destroy runs the real shatter, with debris and the prop’s own On destroyed signals; Reset brings a destroyed one back whole, or tops up a merely damaged one; Push is an impulse away from a place, scaled by each prop’s mass so one number moves a crate and a barrel the same amount. None of them edit the saved level.
Spawn prefabInstantiate a saved prefab at a place — a tycoon’s "buy → building appears", a buildable turret, a farming drop. Capped at 200 live spawned props; destroyed ones give the budget back.
Set prop valueA named number on a prop itself rather than on the level.

Every stat is a multiplier of what the level authored, which means ×1 always puts it back exactly — there's no hidden “original value” to lose track of. Move speed ×0.4 is a swamp, Gravity ×0.3 is the moon, Jump ×2 plus Gravity ×0.5 is a floaty platformer. Applying the same rule twice never compounds, so a repeating trigger can't slow you to a standstill. Max health is a multiple of the engine's baseline 100 (×1.5 = 150), and raising the ceiling doesn't secretly heal you. A stat springs back at the start of every match — and only the stats a rule actually touched are reset, so a +25 max HP perk keeps its bonus.

A “place” is a tag. Type or pick one and it resolves to the prop carrying it — the same naming system the rest of the signal system uses, so you point at something you can see and select in the viewport. Two names are built in: me (blank means the same thing) is wherever the player is standing, and start is the level's player start. A trigger volume answers to its own event name, so the zone that fires a rule can also be the spot it acts on — On event reachedVault → Spawn 4 Brutes at reachedVault. If several props share a tag, each use picks one at random, so “spawn at guardPost” scatters a patrol across all of them. A place nobody carries does nothing — the verb is skipped rather than firing at the world origin.

The advanced gates

  • Needs N different senders — the receiving prop only reacts once N different props have signalled it (the same sender firing twice counts once). Both pressure plates; all three generators.
  • Needs item — a rule can require the player to be carrying an inventory item, with an optional consume: “On E, if carrying the fuse, do Open — and spend the fuse.” A locked prop without the item shows a LOCKED toast naming what's missing.
  • On object placed extras — filter by a from tag (only the tagged object triggers it, blank = any physical prop), choose Touching it vs Inside it, and optionally consume the placed object (it vanishes when it lands — sacrificial offerings).

Locks & keys

The Lock fold requires a key (red / blue / gold / green) before E works on the prop, with optional Consume key on unlock. Keys are granted by key pickup pads (one-shot — they don't respawn), appear as HUD chips, and can carry a custom display name set on the pickup.

SystemsPoint & click — adventures, tycoons, RTS

Gameplay tab ▸ Player options · Object ▸ Signals ▸ On click

The mouse can reach the world. A prop can answer a click from across the room, the cursor and the crosshair both say what is clickable, and the player can be sent walking to wherever you click the floor. That is the whole toolkit behind a point-and-click adventure, a tycoon, an ARPG or an RTS — and none of it needs the Logic graph unless you want it.

The two switches

Both live on the Gameplay tab, in the Player options fold — and they are offered only in Top-down, Side-scroll, and in Third person once ARPG cursor aim is on. In first person the mouse is the camera, so the pair is not shown at all and a hint in its place says where it went.

SettingWhat it does
Free mouse cursorThe pointer is no longer captured. You get a real arrow you can move anywhere on screen, and clicking aims where the pointer is instead of where the camera looks. Required for anything point-and-click.
Click to moveClicking the floor walks the player there, routed around obstacles by the engine's own navigation. Only available with the free cursor on, because it needs a pointer to click the floor with. Any movement key takes control straight back.
You do not need the free cursor to use On click. In first person the crosshair is the pointer: clicking fires the ray through screen centre, and a ring appears around the reticle when it rests on something clickable. So a clickable world works in every view — the free cursor just changes how you aim at it.

How to: make a prop clickable

  1. Select the prop. Open Signals.
  2. + Add signal, and set when to On click.
  3. Pick a do — Toggle a door, → Logic event to run a rule, Open/close a modal to show a menu, anything in the verb table.
  4. Give the prop a Name (top of Object & selection). The name is what the player reads when they point at it, so “Vault Door” beats “box 14”.
  5. Press ▶ Play and point at it.

What the player gets, free, with no extra authoring:

  • The cursor becomes a hand over anything clickable (free-cursor views).
  • A ring appears around the crosshair when it rests on something clickable (first person). It is the same answer, drawn where you are actually aiming.
  • The prompt names it — CLICK Vault Door — using the prop's Name. If you are standing close enough for an E prompt, that one wins, because the thing at arm's reach is the actionable one.

How to: click-to-move

  1. Player tab ▸ View & camera ▸ tick Free mouse cursor, then Click to move.
  2. Play, and click the floor.

A ring pings at the destination in your HUD accent colour and holds there while you walk, vanishing when you arrive. Click somewhere the player cannot route to and the ring pings red and does not hold — so a refused click never looks like a click the game ignored. Clicking something with an On click signal runs that signal instead of walking; a click is one thing or the other, never both.

Routing is the engine's own navigation, the same one the bots use — so the player walks around walls, up ramps and onto upper storeys rather than into them. If a route stops making progress (a door closed across it, a crate rolled into it) it is abandoned rather than pushed at forever.

How to: a working prize counter

The full loop — click a thing, a menu opens, a button spends a variable — in six steps:

  1. Place a prop. Name it Prize Counter.
  2. Signals ▸ On click ▸ Open/close a modal ▸ Open… ▸ modal name shop, and tick freeze the world so the match stops while the menu is up.
  3. HUD tab ▸ + Widget ▸ kind Button. Label Buy a prize, in modal shop, fires event buy.
  4. Add a second widget, kind Text, in modal shop, label Tickets {tickets}.
  5. Gameplay tab ▸ Open logic graph. Add On event named buy → Change variable tickets by -1.
  6. Play, click the counter, press the button.

The Preview dropdown on the HUD tab shows that modal's widgets on screen while you lay them out, so you are not authoring blind. It is a view, not a setting: it is never saved and never opens the modal in play, and show when is ignored inside it so nothing is invisible while you arrange it.

If a button does nothing, the Level check will tell you. A control that fires an event no On event node listens for is reported by name in Save ▸ Level check — as are a modal nothing opens, and a modal that opens onto no widgets.

SystemsVisual logic — the Logic graph

The signal system wires one cause to one effect. The Logic graph (Gameplay tab ▸ Logic graph ▸ Open logic graph) is the layer above it: a full-screen node canvas where you build real game logic — counters, timers, branches, variables, randomness, loops and reusable functions — without writing code. Drag the board to pan, wheel to zoom, drag nodes by their headers, drag an output port onto an input port to wire them, click a wire to remove it. Tag, event-name, animation-clip and sound fields are drop-downs: they list every tag your props and lights already carry (with a count), every event name declared anywhere, every clip name your placed models ship, and every sound URL the level already uses — pick from the list or type a brand-new value.

Reusable functions are the On event / Send event pair: build a chain once behind a named event and call it from anywhere — the calling chain continues after the call. A classic graph: On start → Set score = 0; a pressure plate fires → Logic event "scored" → Change score +1 → Branch score ≥ 5 → Show message → Win level.

Enemy hooks

The AI had exactly one connection to the graph: On enemy killed. Two more entries close the loop.

NodeFires when
On enemy spots youAn enemy goes from oblivious to alerted — once per alert, not every frame it's hunting you. It covers every way that happens: seeing you, hearing gunfire, catching a blast, or a rule telling it to. It re-arms when the enemy gives up, so the next time it notices you it fires again.
On enemy damagedAn enemy takes a hit and survives it — a boss phase change at half health, a retreat, an alarm. A killing blow fires On enemy killed instead; one hit never fires both.

Neither carries which enemy it was, on purpose: pair them with Command enemies (above) and you have the whole classic loop in four nodes — On enemy spots you → Change alarm +1 → Command all enemies: Alert them to vaultDoor. A stealth game is the same loop plus Command enemies: Lose the player on the other side. Both cost nothing until you actually put one on the board.

Values that carry over

Normally every variable resets when a level starts — which quietly rules out anything with progress: a running coin total, a quest stage, a shortcut you unlocked. Under Gameplay ▸ Logic graph, below the Open button, every variable name in the level is listed with a tick box. Tick one and it survives into the next level of the campaign.

What happensWhen
The carried value is put back into the variableAt the start of every match, before On start runs — so your first node can already read it
The live value is carried forwardWhen the level is cleared, and when a Go to level doorway is taken
The value rewinds to what you walked in withOn a retry — the level was not cleared, so nothing was committed
Everything resets to zeroWhen you start the campaign from the top, or hit Clear in the panel

That asymmetry is on purpose: a player can never lose progress by dying, and can never farm a level by replaying the first half of it. A variable you tick but never write settles at 0 rather than nothing, so a Branch on it is never comparing against a blank.

The tick means “this room passes its value on” — it is not a gate on what arrives. Anything the campaign is already carrying lands in every room whether or not that room ticked it, so a room you forgot to tick cannot end a run. The panel lists those names unticked, and the carrying now: line under it says exactly what is being held.

Also keep them between sessions is a separate opt-in below the list. With it on, the carried values are written to the player's browser, so they can close the tab and come back to the same progress; with it off — the default — nothing is stored anywhere. The panel always shows what is being carried right now, with one button to throw it away.

The graph runs on the host (its world actions broadcast through the signal system, so multiplayer stays in sync — spawned enemies and pickups stream in the world snapshot, and a teleport, damage or heal aimed at “the player” reaches every teammate, as do given items, bent stats and a music change) and pauses while the editor is open — playtest to see it run. Variables and timers reset at every match start. A mis-wired infinite loop can't freeze the game: the graph stops for that frame and a toast tells you to add a Delay.

SystemsInterfaces — widgets & menus

HUD tab → Custom widgets (logic-driven)

Everything on screen that is not the engine's own HUD is built here. A widget is one element — some text, a bar, a countdown, a button, a picture. Widgets read your Logic variables and a Button fires a logic event back, so the HUD is a two-way surface, not a readout. Give several widgets the same modal name and they become a menu that opens and closes as one: a shop, an upgrade screen, a card hand, a pause-style panel of your own.

The five widget kinds

KindWhat it showsFields that matter
+ TextA line of text. Anything in curly braces is a live variable: SCORE {score}, {coins} coins · {lives} lives.label
+ TimerA number of seconds, formatted. Point it at the variable a Countdown node fills.value (the variable), format
+ BarA fill bar — value ÷ max. Max can be a plain number or another variable, so a bar can grow as well as drain.value, max

+ ImageA picture from a URL — a card face, a portrait, a panel frame, a logo. image URL, width/height, opacity

Every widget also has: anchor (nine screen positions) plus an offset in pixels, size, theme colour (untick to pick your own), panel (the plate behind it), show when, and in modal. The image URL field exists on every kind, not just Image — on the others it is a backdrop, so a Button can be a card face and a Bar can sit inside a frame you drew.

Show when takes a variable name. The widget is hidden while that variable is zero or unset, and appears the moment it is anything else. Leave it blank for “always”. So a Set variable node is all it takes to pop a banner, reveal a scoreboard at the halfway mark, or show a “PRESS E” prompt only when the player is holding the key.

Stacking — which widget covers which

Widgets draw in list order, last in front. Use ▼ back / ▲ front on a widget's card to restack. Put a panel frame or a card face at the back and the text and buttons over it.

An Image never blocks a Button's clicks. That is deliberate — art is art — but it means a picture drawn over a button hides a control that still works. If a click seems to land on nothing, check whether something is stacked over it.

Counting down

You do not need arithmetic for a timer. The Logic graph has a Countdown node (Flow category) with three modes:

StartBegin counting down into a variable, from sec seconds.
StopHalt it where it is. The variable keeps its value.
Add timePush more seconds onto a running countdown — a time-extend pickup.

The output is an ordinary logic variable, which is the whole point: a Timer widget shows it, Branch compares it, an expression reads it, and it stops while the game is paused or a cutscene is playing.

Step by step — a 60-second time trial.
  1. Logic tab → add On event, name it runStart.
  2. Add a Countdown node: mode Start, into left, sec 60. Wire On event → it.
  3. Add Branch: left ≤ 0. Wire it from an Every X sec node set to 1 second so it is checked each tick, and its true output into Do action → Lose level.
  4. HUD tab → + Timer, value left, format tenths, anchor top-centre.
  5. On your start-line prop: Signals → When contact → → Logic event named runStart.
Deploy, run through the start line, and the clock runs.

Modals — building a menu

Type a name into a widget's in modal field and it leaves the HUD and joins that menu. Every widget sharing the name opens and closes together, over a dimmed backdrop, with the mouse freed and the world no longer taking clicks. Nothing else has to be set up — the name is the menu.

Open and close it from the Logic graph: Do action → Open/close a modal, then Open… or Close and the modal's name. A prop signal can do the same, so a shop counter you walk up to and press E on opens the shop.

Step by step — a shop that sells a turret.
  1. HUD tab → + Button. Label TURRET — 50, event buyTurret, in modal shop.
  2. + Text, label COINS {coins}, in modal shop, anchored above it.
  3. + Button, label CLOSE, event shopClose, in modal shop.
  4. Set Preview to shop and drag the three into place.
  5. Logic tab: On event buyTurret → Branch coins ≥ 50. On true: Change variable coins −50, then Do action → Spawn prefab at a tag you placed. On false: a Show message node reading “Not enough coins”.
  6. On event shopClose → Do action → Open/close a modal → Close, shop.
  7. On the shop counter prop: Signals → When interacted → Open/close a modal → Open… shop.
That is a working economy: walk up, press E, spend, close.
Widgets and modals save with the level and reach every player in multiplayer — the host's numbers are the truth. A per-player variable (a name ending in @) shows each player their own number in the same widget, which is what a co-op shop needs.

SystemsCampaigns & doorways

Save tab → Campaign · Logic tab → Go to level

A big level does not have to be one file. Split it into rooms — a hub and four wings, a fairground and its booths, a house you walk through — build each as its own level, chain them into a campaign, and put a doorway where they meet. This is how Half-Life and Portal are built, and the pieces are already here.

Chaining the levels

Save tab → Campaign. Build a room, press ➕ Add current level, build the next, add it too. Reorder with ▲/▼, remove with ✕, open one with its ✎ button. ▶ Play campaign runs them in order. Export campaign writes the whole sequence as one file and Import campaign reads it back — that is what you share.

Every room is a complete level. Its own win condition ends it and loads the next, so a linear campaign needs no logic at all. Doorways are for when you want to move between rooms in any order — a hub, a shortcut, a door you can walk back through.

Doorways — the Go to level node

Logic tab → Go to level (in Actions). Three fields:

FieldWhat it does
level #Which room to load, counting from 1 — the numbers you see in the Campaign list. It takes a variable name too, so a graph can compute the destination.
arrive at tagA tag on a prop in the destination room. The player materialises there, facing the way that prop faces. Leave it blank and they arrive at that room's own player start.
keep gearTick it and weapons, ammo and health cross the threshold unchanged. Untick it and the destination's own loadout applies.
Which way is the marker facing? Rotate the arrival prop in the destination room and the player arrives looking that way — the prop's forward is the arrival facing. Point it into the room, not at the wall you just came through.
keep gear is the difference between a room split out of one level and a hub world. A corridor into the next room of the same building should keep it on: losing your shotgun at a doorway is a bug as far as the player is concerned. A hub that hands out a fresh loadout per wing wants it off.
The destination tag lives in the other level, so the field offers no autocomplete — there is nothing in this level to suggest. Type it carefully; a tag nothing answers to drops the player at the room's own start and reports it in Level check.

  2. Build Room B. Place an arrival prop tagged fromLobby, rotated to face into the room. Save and add it too — it is level 2.

Step by step — a hub with four wings. Build the hub as level 1 and each wing as 2–5. In the hub, put a tagged plinth at each doorway with a trigger zone in front of it, and wire each to Go to level with that wing's number and keep gear off so each wing issues its own kit. In each wing, one trigger at the exit goes back to level 1, arriving at a tag beside the door the player left by — so returning puts them where they expect, not at the hub's spawn. Tick a wingsDone variable to carry, add Change variable wingsDone +1 at each wing's exit, and a Branch in the hub can open the finale at 4.

Progress across sessions

Under the carry list: Also keep them between sessions writes the carried values into the player's browser, so they can close the tab and come back. Two more boxes appear under it once that is on — Carry the inventory too and Resume at the last checkpoint. Both are solo only, and finishing the game clears the checkpoint so the next run starts clean.

Doorways are solo and host-only. In co-op, a client does not follow the host through a door — build multiplayer levels as one room, or as a campaign whose rooms end on their own win conditions.

Rules that shape the run

  • Objective text — the goal banner (players re-read it with J; signals can rewrite it mid-run).
  • Respawn at checkpoint (solo) — death returns you to the last checkpoint instead of ending the run.
  • Wave mode — Random, or Pre-built from your spawn markers (optionally single-pass, no respawns); between-wave upgrade cards in solo.
  • Fall damage — on/off for player and AI, with safe speed and damage scaling. Ragdoll on death. Crushing props — heavy movers deal damage, with min speed.
  • Camera view — how players see your level: First person (the classic), Third-person, Top-down, or Side-scroller. Saved with the level, so it carries through share links, the community gallery and multiplayer.
    • Third-person — an over-the-shoulder chase camera that smoothly follows the player with damping (it eases after sprints, jumps and turns instead of being bolted rigid; aiming tightens it back to exact). Controls and aim stay first-person-style. Tune the framing and the Follow smoothing slider under Player tab → Third-person framing — smoothing is also a player preference, so it applies when anyone toggles third-person from the pause menu too.
    • Top-down — a fixed isometric-tilt camera follows the player; WASD moves on the map (screen-relative), and the mouse steers a cursor: your character faces it, shots hit whatever is under it, and grenades lob onto it (up to the grenade's throw-force range), twin-stick style. Cars keep their full handling — instant chase/racing levels. Camera distance sets the zoom (8–80).
    • Side-scroller — the camera locks to one side and players run along a single lane (pick east–west or north–south). A/D run, Space jumps, and the mouse aims anywhere within the plane (grenades arc to the cursor too). Players are held on the lane they deploy on, so build your level along it — ramps and stairs shine here.
    • Camera framing (build 1085) — both fixed views orbit the player, and every part of the orbit is yours:
      ControlWhat it does
      Camera distanceHow far back the camera orbits (top 8–80, side 6–60)
      RotateSwings the camera around the player. Top-down: full 360°, and WASD rotates with it so W is always up-screen. Side-scroller: ±60° for an angled 2.5D look — the player still runs along the lane.
      TiltAngle above the horizon. Top-down 15°–89° (89 is almost straight down, low is strongly isometric); side-scroller −30°–60°, so you can look down on the lane or up at it.

SystemsCutscenes — shot by shot

Gameplay tab → Cutscenes. A cutscene is a list of camera shots played back-to-back with hard cuts; each shot flies through its own waypoints. Cutscene #1 is the intro (toggle Play intro flythrough on deploy); every additional cutscene is named and fired by a signal's Play cutscene action. Any key or tap skips playback.

Waypoints: fly the editor camera to a spot, click + Add camera waypoint (current view), repeat — 2+ points move the camera; a single point is a held shot. Per point: Go to (jump the editor view there), Set (overwrite with the current view), 👁 (aim the editor at your subject, then click to store a look-target), ⊘ (clear it), ✕ (delete).

ExtrasRecipes — things built end to end

Each of these is a complete thing you can build in one sitting, using nothing that is not in this manual. They are written as sequences to follow, not as ideas to take away.

Building a shooting range

Plates that score when you hit them, never disappear, and reset on demand. This is the recipe the engine's own 🎯 Shootable target flag exists for.

  1. Build the plate. Add a box, scale it flat and stand it up. Open Object & selection → Physics & destruction and tick 🎯 Shootable target. Do not tick Dynamic — the plate should stay bolted where you put it.
  2. Make it score, not shatter. In the same fold, untick Breakable. It now takes hits forever: the flash, the sound and the signal all still fire, it just never breaks.
  3. Tag it. Give it the tag plate1. Duplicate it twice along the booth and tag the copies plate2 and plate3.
  4. Wire the hit. On each plate: Signals → + Add signal → When hit → → Logic event named plateHit.
  5. Count it. Logic tab → On event plateHit → Change variable score +1.
  6. Show the score. Either a HUD widget (+ Text, label SCORE {score}) or a Sign on the back wall reading SCORE {score}. The sign is the nicer one for a fairground.
  7. Add a reset. Place a lever prop, tick Interactable, and give it a signal: When E pressed → Reset props, tag plate1. Repeat for the others, or tag all three plate and use one signal — a tag names a set.
Why untick Breakable? Because the label means “ever destroyed”, not “ever damaged”. A plate that shatters on the third hit stops being a range after three shots. If you want a plate that breaks, leave it on and use Reset props to bring it back between rounds.
Make it a timed round. Add a start line: a trigger zone whose event name starts an On event chain that starts a Countdown into left for 30 seconds, sets score to 0, and shows a Timer widget. A Branch on left ≤ 0 fires a Show message node reading Time! You hit {score}.

Building a movement course

An obstacle run that teaches its own verbs. Every switch below is on the World tab under Movement.

  1. Turn the verbs on. Set Air jumps to 1 (a double jump), Wall jump to about 14, and Air dash to about 22. Leave Tap hop at 0.5 so a short press gives a short hop.
  2. Teach them in order. Build the course in four stretches, each of which can only be crossed with one verb: a gap too wide for one jump (air jump), a chimney of two facing walls (wall jump), a long flat gap with no wall (air dash), and a low ceiling you have to tap-hop under.
  3. Sign each stretch. A Sign at the entrance to each — “Jump again in mid-air”, “Jump off the wall”. Nobody reads a manual; everybody reads a sign in front of them.
  4. Add checkpoints. A trigger zone at each stretch's start, each firing its own event name, and an On event → Do action → Set checkpoint for each. Falling then costs seconds, not the whole run.
  5. Time it. Exactly the timed round above, with the format set to hundredths so two runs can be compared.
Build the gaps, then walk them. A jump that looks right is often half a metre out — Deploy, run it, come back and nudge. The play-from-here button drops you at the editor camera so you can retest one gap without running the course.

A room with someone in it

  1. Import or place a character prop, and open Dialogue (NPC). Give it a Name and three lines.
  2. Tick Interactable so the player gets a prompt.
  3. In a dialogue line, write {set metGuard = 1} — the conversation now leaves a mark on the world.
  4. Put a locked door across the exit: on the door prop, Signals → When E pressed → Open, and set the needs count beside the Tag box (“different sender(s) before it reacts”) if it should wait on more than one thing.
  5. Logic tab: On event from the guard's last line → Do action → Open, tag the door.
  6. Under Values that carry over, tick metGuard so the next room knows.

Stitching booths into a fairground

Once each booth works on its own, a campaign turns them into a place.

  1. Save each booth as its own level and add them to the campaign in order — the hub first.
  2. In the hub, put a tagged plinth at each booth's entrance and a trigger zone in front of it, wired to Go to level with that booth's number and keep gear on.
  3. In each booth, one trigger at the exit goes back to level 1, arriving at a tag beside the door the player came out of.
  4. Tick a booths variable to carry, add Change variable booths +1 when each booth is completed, and a Branch in the hub opens the finale at your booth count.
  5. Put a Text widget reading {booths} / 4 booths cleared in the hub so the player always knows where they are.
Doorways are solo. If the fairground should be co-op, keep it as one level and use trigger zones to move the player between areas instead.

ExtrasTips & troubleshooting

Using a big imported model as a whole level? (build 1089) The collision shape for an imported model is built by voxelising it into columns, so doorways and archways stay passable. Before build 1089 that builder gave up on anything over 200,000 triangles and fell back to one solid box per mesh — on architecture, where a single mesh is a whole building, that made every building a solid block. Enemies were pushed out of them constantly while the player walked through unaffected (the player's movement samples the real mesh and steps out of over-sized boxes; the enemy resolve did not). The builder now sizes itself to the model and handles level-scale geometry, and enemies get the same step allowance the player has. If you built a level on an older build and enemies stuck in mid-air, just re-open and re-save it.
  • Sketchfab search says it needs a token — it's a free API key: sketchfab.com → Settings → Password & API. Paste it once in any search box; every other box switches to “Token saved · change”.
  • A shared level is missing models — Sketchfab downloads require each player's own token; Poly Pizza models need none. Prefer Poly Pizza for levels you plan to share widely.
  • The game looks out of date — hard-refresh (Ctrl+Shift+R) and check the build number bottom-left of the home menu.
  • Performance dips — post-FX sheds passes automatically under load, but the biggest wins are yours: fewer point lights, smaller textures, fewer high-poly Sketchfab models. Adaptive resolution (pause menu) trades sharpness for frame rate on sustained drops — turn it off if you'd rather keep full resolution and accept the dips.
  • Edges look jagged / aliased — check Adaptive resolution in the pause menu: when the frame rate struggles it lowers the render resolution, which reads as shimmer on edges; untick it for full sharpness. (Since build 872, Visual effects keeps proper antialiasing — older builds lost it whenever effects were on.)
  • My race rivals won't spawn — rivals need a closed track loop (use Close loop) and a placed drivable vehicle to clone. The Save tab's issues panel calls this out.
  • A mechanism won't fire — check its Trigger: Signals only waits for a signal; E Activate may be gated by a Lock. The signal's target tag must match exactly.
  • I can't find a setting — sections remember their collapsed state; expand the section headers. Selection folds (Vehicle, Signals…) only appear with a prop selected on the Build tab.
